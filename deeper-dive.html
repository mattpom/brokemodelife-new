Lazy Summer Days: The Case for Doing Less (And Why It Actually Works)

Summer comes loaded with pressure to make the most of it: trips to plan, lists of things to see, a calendar that somehow gets busier once school lets out. But some of the best summer days are the ones with nothing on them at all. A slow afternoon on a porch, a nap in the shade, an hour spent staring at the water with no particular goal -- these are not failures to make the most of the season. They may be exactly what makes the season worth having.

Myth: Doing Nothing Is a Waste of a Day

There's a quiet guilt that shows up on unstructured days, the sense that time not spent producing or achieving something is time wasted. That belief doesn't hold up well under examination. Unstructured downtime gives the brain room to shift out of task mode, and that shift is where a lot of the restorative benefit of rest actually comes from. A lazy afternoon isn't the absence of a productive day. It's doing a different, quieter kind of work: recovering from the rest of the year.

Myth: You Need a Big Trip to Actually Relax

Relaxation gets marketed as something that requires a flight, a resort, or at minimum a full day of planning. In reality, the ingredients for a genuinely restful afternoon are much simpler: enough unscheduled time, a comfortable place to be, and permission to not accomplish anything. A hammock in the backyard or a blanket in the local park can do more for a person's sense of ease than an expensive trip squeezed into a rigid itinerary. The goal isn't distance from home. It's distance from obligation.

The Science Behind Slowing Down

Research backs up the idea that free time genuinely helps, but with a twist: more isn't automatically better. A study published by the American Psychological Association, led by Wharton's Marissa Sharif, analyzed tens of thousands of Americans' daily schedules and found that well-being rose as discretionary time increased, leveling off at around two hours and beginning to decline after about five. In follow-up experiments, people who imagined having a moderate amount of free time each day reported higher well-being than those who imagined having either very little or a very large amount. The people with too much unstructured time felt less productive, which dragged their well-being back down. The takeaway isn't that rest doesn't matter. It's that a well-placed lazy afternoon, not an entire lazy season, tends to do the most good.

Why Rest Is Harder to Come By Than It Sounds

If slowing down is this useful, it's worth asking why so many people don't do it. Part of the answer is structural. According to Pew Research Center, 46 percent of U.S. workers who receive paid time off take less of it than their employer actually offers them, and the most common reasons are feeling they don't need it or worrying about falling behind at work. In other words, a large share of the unused rest available to American workers isn't gone because people don't want it. It's gone because guilt and workplace pressure get in the way of taking it. That makes intentionally protecting a lazy summer day less of a small indulgence and more of a genuine correction to a pattern most people are stuck in.

How to Actually Have a Lazy Summer Day

Protecting unstructured time takes more effort than it sounds like it should, mostly because the rest of life doesn't leave open space on its own. A few approaches make it easier to actually claim a slow day instead of just hoping one shows up.

The no-plan afternoon means blocking out a chunk of time, an afternoon or a full day, with nothing scheduled into it on purpose, then resisting the urge to fill it once it arrives. The point isn't to do nothing productive with the time; it's to let whatever happens in it happen without an agenda.

The short unplug works well for people who can't clear a whole day. Setting phone notifications aside, or leaving the phone in another room entirely, for even two or three hours removes the low-grade pull to check in on work or the news, which is often what keeps a supposedly relaxing day from actually feeling relaxing.

The near-home reset uses a nearby park, backyard, porch, or pool instead of a destination that requires travel and planning. Removing the logistics of "going somewhere" removes most of the friction that turns a relaxing idea into a stressful errand.

None of these require a free schedule for the whole summer. A single afternoon a week, protected on purpose, does more than an occasional day off that gets swallowed by errands or screens.

Rest Doesn't Have to Be Perfect

It's worth being honest that not every lazy day will feel restorative. Some will get interrupted, some will drift into scrolling instead of genuine rest, and some will just feel boring rather than peaceful. That doesn't make the attempt pointless. The research suggests it's the pattern of regularly protecting some unstructured time, not any single perfect day, that adds up to better well-being. A slow afternoon that gets half-derailed still beats a summer with none at all.

The Bottom Line

Lazy summer days aren't a lesser version of a well-used season. They're a documented, evidence-backed way to support well-being, and one that a large share of people aren't actually using, whether because of guilt, workplace pressure, or the assumption that rest needs to be earned or elaborate. A porch, a few unscheduled hours, and permission to do nothing in particular are enough. The rest tends to take care of itself.

Sources: American Psychological Association, "Having Too Little or Too Much Time Is Linked to Lower Subjective Well-Being," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (Sept 2021); Pew Research Center, "More than 4 in 10 U.S. workers don't take all their paid time off" (Aug 2023).
